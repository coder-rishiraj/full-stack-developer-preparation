Chapter 7

Europe: permits, employment law, GDPR

This chapter does double duty. It is the knowledge you need to get a European job — which permit, which salary floor, which country — and it is the knowledge you need to do one, because a recruiter in Europe who does not understand notice periods, works councils and GDPR will make expensive mistakes in the first month.

A standing caveat for this whole chapter. Thresholds and rules change every year, and several change in 2026. Every figure here is a 2026 planning number with its source named on the plan page. Before you quote a number in an interview or to a candidate you should check the official site — the Irish Department of Enterprise, the Dutch IND, the German Make it in Germany pages. Saying "around €69,000, but I'd confirm the current figure" is professional. Quoting a stale number with confidence is not.

The strategic point. You do not need a job in Europe generally; you need a job at a company that can and will sponsor. That is a much smaller set, and it is identifiable in advance: in the Netherlands the IND publishes the recognised-sponsor register, and in Ireland and Germany the signal is a company that already employs non-EU staff. Filtering for sponsorship before applying is the single biggest saving of effort in the whole search.

The numbers that decide it

Permits, and the salary floors

Three routes matter. Each is really one question: does the offer clear the floor?

Route2026 floorHow it works
Ireland — Critical Skills Employment Permit€68,911 for roles not on the listed-occupations listNo labour-market test, two-year job offer required, faster route to long-term residence. The occupation must not be on the ineligible list — and human resources administrative occupations are, which is why the offer has to be a professional recruiting or analytics role, not "HR admin".

What breaks a hire in Europe

Employment law a recruiter must know

You do not need to be a lawyer. You need to know which questions have legal answers so you escalate instead of guessing, and you need to stop promising things that cannot be delivered.

  • The written contract is the job. European employment is contractual and durable. What the contract says about role, hours, location and notice is binding in a way that an offer email is not. "We'll sort out the details later" is not how this works.
  • Probation is real and it is long. Commonly three to six months. During probation notice is short and ending the contract is straightforward; afterwards, in Germany especially, dismissal becomes genuinely difficult. A manager who treats probation as a formality is in for a surprise, and a candidate who is not told the probation length will feel misled.
  • Notice periods are longer than you are used to. Often one to three months, on both sides, set by contract and by statute. A candidate with three months' notice is completely ordinary. The start date must respect it, and pushing someone to breach notice is a bad trade — it burns a reference and sometimes creates liability.
  • Works councils. In Germany, the Netherlands, Austria and elsewhere, an elected employee body can have formal rights in hiring and in changes to working conditions. In Germany a works council's consent may be needed for a hire. This is an extra step in the process, with its own timing. The correct behaviour is to ask whether one exists and plan around it, never to route around it.
  • Collective agreements. In much of Europe a sector-wide agreement (a Dutch CAO, a German Tarifvertrag) can set minimum pay, hours and leave for the role — above the statutory minimum and outside the company's discretion. If one applies, the salary band is not fully negotiable, and that is useful to know before a negotiation rather than during one.
  • Leave and the extras. Ireland's statutory minimum is four working weeks. The Netherlands pays a holiday allowance of typically 8% on top of salary, and it is excluded from the migrant salary test. Germany's contractual leave is usually well above the legal floor, and a 13th-month payment is common in several countries. Comparing a European offer to an Indian one on base salary alone is misleading in both directions.
  • Right to work is checked before day one. A passport is not a visa. Sponsorship is the employer's application. Verifying this is a compliance step with real consequences, and it is often the recruiter who owns it.
  • Structured interviewing is the norm — the same questions for every candidate, evidence from past work, a score recorded. "I liked them" is not feedback that can be defended if a rejection is challenged, and in a discrimination complaint the written record is the defence.
  • Discrimination law is strict and the protected grounds are broad: age, sex, race, religion, disability, sexual orientation, and in several countries more. Practically: no photographs or dates of birth required on applications, no "native speaker", no questions about family plans, and no age-coded language like "young and dynamic".

The change that is arriving right now

Pay transparency

The EU Pay Transparency Directive must be in national law across member states by June 2026. It is the most significant change to European recruiting practice in years, and being able to discuss it intelligently is a genuine differentiator — most recruiters have heard of it and few can say what it requires.

RequirementWhat it means for a recruiter, concretely
Pay information before interviewCandidates must be given the initial pay level or range for the role. In practice, ranges in adverts and in outreach become standard rather than optional.
No asking about salary historyEmployers may not ask what a candidate currently earns. This is a direct behaviour change: the old "what are you on at the moment?" opener becomes unlawful. The replacement question is "what are you looking for, and does our range work for you?"
Right to information on payEmployees can request the average pay level for their role, broken down by sex.
Gender pay-gap reportingLarger employers must report, and must take joint action if an unexplained gap exceeds 5%. This is analytics work — and it is exactly the kind of reporting your Excel and Power BI skills are for.
Gender-neutral job adverts and titlesAdvert language and the selection process must be non-discriminatory.

From principle to a dated spreadsheet

GDPR, made operational

Chapter 4 covered the eight principles. The question here is the one an interviewer actually asks: what do you do differently because of GDPR? Four concrete answers.

  1. A retention clock on every application. Candidate data cannot be kept indefinitely. Common practice is six to twelve months after a decision for unsuccessful applicants, longer only with explicit consent for a talent pool. The clock starts at the decision, and someone has to run the deletion.
  2. Consent for the talent pool, separately. Applying for one job is not permission to be contacted about others for three years. That needs a separate, recorded, freely-given yes — and a way to withdraw it.
  3. Interview notes are personal data. A subject access request can reach them. This is the strongest practical argument for writing evidence-based notes: "did not give an example of handling conflict" is defensible, "seemed a bit arrogant" is a liability.
  4. Data minimisation at the application form. If a field is not needed to assess the application, it should not be collected. Date of birth, photograph, marital status and nationality on a first application are the classic over-collections, and they are also discrimination risk.

Run the deletion report

This is the work in practice: a list of applications, a retention period, and a date. Six candidates, a retention period in months, and whether they consented to a talent pool. Today is pinned to 1 May 2026 so the answers are stable.

Why this is a good portfolio piece. A one-page retention report — what is collected, why, for how long, what a candidate can ask for, and a dated list of what is due for deletion — is small, entirely fictional, and shows you can turn a legal principle into an operational control. Very few recruiting candidates bring anything like it.

The question that decides the shortlist

Talking about your own visa

It will come up, usually early, often as "are you eligible to work here?" The wrong answers are vagueness and apology. The right answer is a short, factual, confident one that makes sponsoring your look like an ordinary administrative step rather than a risk.

What hurts

"I would need sponsorship, but I'm very flexible and willing to do whatever it takes. I'm hoping a company might be able to help me with the visa."

"Hoping", "help me", "whatever it takes" — it frames your as a problem the company solves as a favour, and it suggests you have not looked into it.

What works

"I need a permit, yes. For a role at this level in Dublin the route is a Critical Skills Employment Permit, which needs a two-year offer at €68,911 or above and no labour-market test — so from your side it is a single application rather than an advertising process. I have the documents ready and I can start within the notice period plus processing. Is sponsoring something you have done before?"

Names the route, names the number, explains what it costs them, states availability, and ends by putting the question back.

And the honest limit. If a company says plainly that it cannot sponsor, that is the end of that conversation and you should not spend three more interviews hoping it changes. The time saved goes into the next application. Filtering for sponsorship early is not pessimism, it is arithmetic.

Before moving on

You should be able to name the three permit routes and their 2026 floors with the caveat that they are reviewed annually, explain why a €60,000 Dublin offer is a weak visa, say what a works council and a collective agreement are and why they change a process, state two requirements of the Pay Transparency Directive including the salary-history ban, give four concrete things you do differently because of GDPR, and answer the sponsorship question in thirty confident seconds.
