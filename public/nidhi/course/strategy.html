Chapter 8

Sound like a partner, not an order-taker

A recruiter is given a requirement and sources against it. A Talent Acquisition Partner looks at the market, resets the hiring manager's expectations, chooses the channels, sizes the funnel, and watches conversion. That sentence is the whole chapter. The lessons below are how to do each part of it with numbers, not adjectives.

The difference that changes the pay

Recruiter versus partner

European employers paying €70,000 are not buying more sourcing hours. They are buying judgement — someone who will tell a manager the search is wrong before six wasted weeks. The table is the same work, described two ways. You should be able to hear which voice you are using.

The sentence to practise until it is boring: "I looked at the market, reset the hiring manager's expectations, chose the channels, sized the funnel, and watched conversion." Every story in an interview should be a version of that sentence with one real example underneath it.

Headcount is a forecast, not a list

Workforce planning: build, buy, borrow

When a manager says "I need five backend engineers by June," the partner's job is to turn that into a plan with dates, risk and a decision. Three levers, always:

Build

Grow someone already inside. Cheaper, slower, keeps knowledge. Works when the skill is adjacent — a backend engineer learning a new language, not a stores clerk becoming a DevOps engineer in six months.

Buy

Hire permanently. Expensive up front, cheapest over three years, and the only option that builds a team. Visa floors, notice periods and onboarding time all sit on this lever.

Borrow

Contractor, agency, statement of work. Fast, expensive per day, knowledge walks out the door. The right answer for a six-month spike or a skill the company will never need twice.

The fourth option, which managers forget, is do less: drop a project, sequence the hires, or move work to a team that already exists. A partner who can say "you cannot have five seniors in Dublin by June at this band" is more useful than one who promises and then misses.

Move the sliders. A manager wants five joiners in Dublin in 90 days. The calculator uses the practice-data conversion rates so the funnel is honest, not optimistic.

The meeting that decides whether the search works

The intake, as an agenda you own

If you start sourcing from a forwarded JD, you have already given the search away. Thirty minutes, in this order, with notes you will send back the same day so the manager cannot later claim they asked for something else.

  1. The job in one sentence, from the manager's mouth, not the JD. "They will own the payments API" is a job. "Must have 5 years of Java, Kafka, AWS, Kubernetes, Terraform and people skills" is a shopping list.
  2. Last good hire, last failed hire. Two names. What each did. This is the real bar.
  3. Must / should / nice. Three columns. If everything is a must, you stop the meeting and does the list again. A search with eight mandatories finds nobody.
  4. False matches. "Who have you already rejected that looked right on paper?" Saves your repeating the same mistake.
  5. Band, flexibility, visa. Written down. In Europe this includes whether the salary clears the permit floor. Unanswered, the search is not ready.
  6. Who interviews, what each stage is for, feedback SLA. Two working days is the default you propose. Silence is the most common reason a good candidate drops.
  7. Channels you will not waste time on. If the manager's cousin's agency has already failed, you need to hear that now.

You end the meeting with a one-page note, not a thank-you email. Role in one sentence, must / should / nice, band, visa, interview loop, SLA, and the date you will bring the first five profiles. If the manager does not reply, the search does not start. That is discipline, not rudeness.

Pay is a research problem

Market mapping and pay benchmarking

A band pulled from "what we paid last year" is how offers get refused. Benchmarking is a short research task with a date on it, not a feeling.

SourceWhat it is good forCatch
Levels.fyi, Glassdoor, Reed, Morgan McKinley, Hays, Robert Walters guidesA range, with a year and a city on itSelf-reported, often six months stale, and agency guides talk the market up. Use two sources, never one.

Candidates you have already spoken toThe most honest number: what someone with this stack in this city will move forTiny sample. Treat it as a signal, not a survey. Never put their number into a public file.
Internal equityWhat the people already in the seat are onIf the external market has moved and internal has not, you have to say so — hiring above the team creates a retention problem you will own later.

A market map is the same idea for people rather than pay: which companies employ this role in this city, roughly how many, whether they are hiring, and who is a false match. For a backend engineer in Dublin that is the banks, the tech employers, the consultancies, and the product companies — not "anyone with engineer in the title."

Why a stranger would join

EVP and candidate experience

EVP — employee value proposition — is the honest answer to "why would someone leave a good job for this one?" If the hiring manager cannot name three true reasons, the outreach will sound like every other recruiter's. The reasons have to be true. "Fast-paced environment" and "competitive salary" are not reasons.

  • Work: the problem they will own, not the tech stack. "You will rebuild how airlines book parts" beats "we use Python."
  • Team: who they sit with, who they learn from. Named people, not "a great culture."
  • Life: location, hybrid, leave, visa, the thing this company actually does better than the one next door.

Candidate experience is the process felt from the other side. The metrics that measure it are time-to-response, stage-to-stage silence, and whether a rejected person would apply again. The cheapest improvements are also the ones European candidates mention in interviews: a same-week first screen, a date for the next step at the end of every call, and a rejection that names a reason.

The test for a job description. Give it to someone who does not work there. If they cannot say in one sentence what they would do on a Tuesday, the JD is a list of tools, not a job. Rewrite it from the intake notes, not from last year's advertisement.

The conversations that actually change a search

The sentences for a hard meeting

A partner is paid for the meetings a recruiter avoids. Three of them, with the number attached so they cannot be dismissed as opinion.

The spec is unhireable

"I ran the search you wrote. Twelve people in Dublin match all eight must-haves, and eight of them are already earning above our band. Here are five people who match six of the eight. Which two requirements do we drop, or do we reopen the band?"

The manager is the delay

"Four candidates have been waiting nine days for feedback. Two have other processes. The dashboard will show this role as paused until the scorecards exist, because I will not keep people warm with nothing to tell them."

The channel is wrong

"LinkedIn produced four of six applicants and both joiners. The agency produced Eva, who reneged, and a €4,000 fee we still owe. I am not briefing another agency role until we know why Eva dropped, and I want the next euro spent on employee referrals."

The forecast will miss

"At our current offer-to-join rate I need twelve offers for five joiners. We have made two in six weeks. Either we change conversion — faster feedback, a real band — or we change the date. I will not keep the June date on the slide."

Before moving on

You should be able to run an intake without notes, size a funnel from a conversion rate instead of a hope, put a date and a source on a pay number, and deliver one of the hard sentences with a figure attached. Extra reps: craft and job labs.
