Excel labs: the rule, the six people, then you type it.

Each block is a full lesson: why the habit exists, the numbers on Asha–Farah, the trap that makes a report lie, the sentence you would say to a hiring manager, a gold-cell exercise, and a quiz. Same invented six people. Skip a quiz only when you can answer it out loud without looking.

Still invented data. Asha, Ben, Cara, Dev, Eva, Farah. If a formula would be written on a real Ferra file, you write it here first, then rebuilds it there — never the other way around.

Convert before you SUM

Numbers stored as text

An ATS export often delivers headcount, days-to-fill and fees as text. They look like numbers. They sit on the left. SUM skips them with no warning. No red triangle. No #VALUE!. The total is simply too small, and a hiring director will treat that as your number.

Excel has three types. A number can be added. Text cannot. A date is a number wearing a costume. Alignment is the five-second test: numbers hug the right, text hugs the left. One left-aligned “4000 ” with a trailing space is enough to break a spend report.

SUM of the raw column is €1,000. The true spend is €8,000. The missing €7,000 is Ben and Eva. The test is =ISNUMBER(B2). The fix is =VALUE(B2) or =B2*1. In a monthly file, Power Query Change Type is the real fix — this lab exists so you can see the bug with your own eyes first.

Interview line. “If a fee column is left-aligned I convert before I total. SUM of text is a silent under-count, not an error.”

Calendar days, working days, a blank that stays blank

Date arithmetic that recruiting actually uses

Time to hire is Accepted − Applied. Time to fill is Joined − role opened (or requisition raised). Time to start includes notice. They are three clocks. Quoting the wrong one in a standup is how a partner loses the room.

A blank date is zero. Zero minus 2 March 2026 is about −46,083. That is why every duration starts with IF(end="","",end-start). Farah has no Accepted date. Your hire-days cell must stay blank, not a huge negative that then poisons AVERAGE.

NETWORKDAYS(start, end) counts Monday–Friday, inclusive of both ends. A hiring manager who says “that included Easter” wants this. EDATE(Joined, 6) is probation end — Asha joined 20 Apr 2026, so 20 Oct 2026. EOMONTH(date, 0) is month-end for a snapshot. This course’s clock is pinned to 1 May 2026.

Interview line. “Time to hire is the candidate’s wait. Time to fill is the seat’s wait. I never average a blank as zero.”

A share of total that survives fill-down

Dollar signs, again, until they are boring

A share is this row divided by one grand total. When you fill down, Excel walks every reference unless you pin it. LinkedIn 4 / total 6 = 67%. If the total becomes B7, B8, B9 — empty cells — row 3 is #DIV/0!. That is the whole lesson in one broken cell.

B2/$B$6 locks both column and row. F4 (Mac: fn+F4 or Cmd+T) cycles the four states while editing. Mixed refs (B$6, $B6) matter when you copy across months. For a share-of-total column, both dollars.

Fill-down is the test, not the first cell. If O2 looks right and O3 dies, the dollars walked. The three shares here must add to 100%.

Interview line. “A percentage of total uses an absolute reference so fill-down cannot chase an empty cell.”

Buckets without a four-deep nest

IF, then IFS

Two outcomes: IF. Three or four: IFS, first true wins, last test TRUE as the catch-all. More than four: a lookup table. Never a Christmas tree of IF(IF(IF.

Order matters because the first true test wins. If you write Slow before Normal, nobody is ever Normal. On durations: blank if there is no date; otherwise Fast ≤20, Normal ≤35, else Slow. Thresholds are a judgement you can defend. A −46,000 for Farah is not a judgement, it is a bug.

Monday’s list, written by the sheet

AND: still open, and old enough to chase

AND is TRUE only when every test is true. OR is TRUE if any is. They live inside IF. The Monday formula is:

=IF(AND([@Outcome]="Open", TODAY()-[@Applied]>14), "CHASE", "")

TODAY in this course is 1 May 2026. Farah applied 20 March — 42 days, never screened. You are the only chase. Asha joined; Ben declined; Cara was rejected; Dev joined; Eva reneged. None of them is Open, so none of them is a chase even if they were slow.

Do not write =AND(Candidates[Outcome]="Open") expecting a column of flags. AND of a whole column collapses to one TRUE/FALSE. Per-row tests belong in a helper column or in COUNTIFS.

Interview line. “My ageing column is still-open AND past a chase threshold. The sheet writes Monday’s list.”

The funnel, counted properly

COUNT vs COUNTA vs COUNTIF vs COUNTIFS

COUNT of a date column is “how many times this stage happened”. COUNTA of a name column is “how many rows”. If someone typed “pending” in Offer, COUNTA counts a stage that never happened. COUNT does not. That is why funnel stages use COUNT of dates.

COUNTIF is one filter. COUNTIFS is several, all of them, AND not OR. There is no COUNTIFS-OR. For “Declined or Reneged” add two COUNTIFs, or flag a helper column.

Money with a condition

SUMIFS argument order

SUMIFS: sum range first, then pairs of test-range and criteria. SUMIF is the other way round. Write SUMIFS even for one condition so the habit never flips when a second filter appears (Source and Outcome).

Agency spend here is Eva only: €4,000. Eva accepted, then reneged. The fee is still owed. That is the politically useful number — not “we used an agency” but “€4,000 sat on a start that did not happen.” SUM of the whole fee column is the same €4,000 because nobody else carried a fee. The labelled SUMIFS is what you put on a slide.

Interview line. “Agency cost is SUMIFS on fees, not a typed total, and a renege still counts as spend.”

Mean, median, max — pick on purpose

What an average hides

Hire days for the three who accepted: Asha 23, Dev 32, Eva 39. Mean 31.3, median 32, max 39. AVERAGEIFS of those who Joined drops Eva and becomes 27.5. Two true numbers, two stories. An interviewer who knows TA will ask which you are quoting.

AVERAGE ignores blanks, which is why Farah does not drag the mean to zero — if the helper column is truly blank. If the helper holds −46000, AVERAGE is garbage. Blank first, then average.

Quote median and max together on time-to-fill: “typical 32, worst 39”. Never quote MIN alone — the fastest hire is often an internal move or a lucky referral, not a process you can scale.

Interview line. “I pair median with the max. A mean of 31 hides Eva’s 39 and Farah who never accepted.”

Undefined is not zero

Rates that survive a zero denominator

Acceptance = accepts / offers = 3/4 = 75%. Joining = joiners / accepts = 2/3 ≈ 67%. Yield = joiners / applicants = 2/6 ≈ 33%. Three different questions. Mixing them is how a dashboard lies.

A role with no offers yet is blank, not 0%. Wrap with IFERROR or test the denominator. 0% would mean “we offered and everyone said no”, which is a different story from “we have not offered yet”. Calculate the ratio (0.75) and format as percent. Do not multiply by 100 and then format as percent or you get 7500%.

Interview line. “Undefined is blank. Zero percent means we offered and lost them.”

Facts live once

XLOOKUP with a not-found that is filterable

Look for this row’s RoleID in Roles[RoleID], bring back HiringManager, and if the key is missing return the text No role — not #N/A, not a blank. Then you can filter the column to “No role” and get a worklist. Fourth argument is not optional in a professional file.

BE-1 is Maya Shah, Dublin. FE-1 is Leo Berger, Amsterdam. On the practice file Asha, Ben, Cara and Farah are BE-1 (Maya). Dev and Eva are FE-1 (Leo). The manager is a property of the role, typed once. If Maya’s name is misspelled, you fix it on Roles and every candidate row updates.

XLOOKUP looks any direction, names the return column, exact-matches by default. That is why it replaced VLOOKUP. If Excel is old, INDEX/MATCH is the fallback — next block.

Interview line. “Manager name lives on Roles once. Candidates only store RoleID. A missing key returns No role so I can filter the mess.”

Read the files you will inherit

INDEX / MATCH, from the inside out

MATCH(key, column, 0) returns the position (0 = exact). INDEX(return_column, position) fetches that item. Nest MATCH inside INDEX. Read inside-out, always. MATCH runs first.

Always pass 0 to MATCH. Approximate match (1 or omitted) on unsorted RoleIDs is how a plausible wrong manager gets into a report — BE-1 might match the nearest text, not the real row. VLOOKUP has the same trap: last argument FALSE or 0, never missing.

You write XLOOKUP. You read INDEX/MATCH because every inherited file is full of it. Interview answer: “XLOOKUP because it is exact by default and does not break when someone inserts a column. On older Excel I use INDEX/MATCH.”

Interview line. “MATCH finds the row, INDEX returns the field. The zero means exact.”

Spaces are not decoration

TRIM, then map the typo

TRIM removes leading, trailing and doubled spaces. It does not fix “Linkdin”. That needs SUBSTITUTE or a mapping table and XLOOKUP. LEN is the diagnostic: a word that looks like 8 characters and reports 9 has an invisible extra.

PROPER("LINKEDIN") gives “Linkedin”, not “LinkedIn” — so for a known list, map, don’t guess case. COUNTIF is case-insensitive, so “linkedin” still matches “LinkedIn”, but a trailing space does not. The space is the silent killer.

One TRIM column is fine once. A monthly export needs Power Query, where Trim and Replace Values replay every refresh. Do not spend a career retyping this.

Interview line. “I TRIM every text column on an export, then map known typos. I never trust a source mix until that is done.”

One cell, two facts

Split title from city

Do not count characters. “Backend Engineer - Dublin” is not always 18 characters before the hyphen — a different title breaks LEFT(A2,18). Find the separator, then take everything before and after.

TEXTBEFORE(text," - ") and TEXTAFTER if you have Microsoft 365. The SEARCH version still appears in inherited files: =TRIM(LEFT(A2,SEARCH("-",A2)-1)). TRIM after the split catches a space next to the hyphen.

Once title and city are columns, you can COUNTIFS city, XLOOKUP a city-to-country table, and chart mix. While they are glued, nothing groups.

Interview line. “I split on the delimiter, I do not count letters. Power Query Split Column is the monthly version.”

A headline that rewrites itself

TEXTJOIN and TEXT

Joining a number into a sentence drops its format: ="Rate "&0.75 is “Rate 0.75”. TEXT(0.75,"0%") is “75%”. Dates need TEXT(date,"dd mmm yyyy") or a slide says 46083.

TEXTJOIN with ignore-blanks TRUE skips empty names so the open-list does not end in a stray comma. FILTER inside TEXTJOIN is how a dashboard headline lists whoever is still Open without your typing Farah.

Interview line. “A dashboard title is a formula. TEXT keeps the percent sign; TEXTJOIN lists the open names.”

The Monday list, as a spill

FILTER, UNIQUE, the # operator

FILTER returns every matching name. UNIQUE of Source spills the distinct channels. SORT around UNIQUE so Agency, LinkedIn, Referral stay in a stable order for a chart.

N2# means “the whole spill that starts at N2”, so COUNTIFS can sit beside UNIQUE and grow when a fourth source appears. #SPILL! means something is in the way below — delete or move it. Dynamic arrays cannot live inside a Table — they sit beside it.

This is Monday’s open list as one formula, and a source mix that does not need a pivot. When the data grows, the spill grows. That is the point.

Interview line. “UNIQUE plus COUNTIFS on the spill is a source table that updates itself. FILTER is the live worklist.”

Flag, do not delete

Duplicates on a name are not proof

COUNTIF of the name range against this row, greater than 1, returns DUPLICATE. Two Ashas might be two people, or one person on two roles. The key in real life is email or candidate ID. Never Remove Duplicates on a live candidate file without a copy — it deletes permanently.

Lock the range: $A$2:$A$8. If the range walks, row 8 compares against a shifting window and flags the wrong people. A duplicate flag is a question for a human, not a delete key.

Interview line. “I flag duplicates on email, not name, and I never Remove Duplicates on a live ATS extract.”

No typed KPI

A six-cell dashboard that only points

Applicants, screened, offers, accepts, acceptance rate, agency spend. Every cell a formula. If you can rebuild this from a blank sheet in five minutes, chapter 1 is done.

The six cells must point at the table. If you type 6, 5, 4, 3, 75%, 4000 the dashboard is a photograph of one Tuesday. Add a seventh candidate and it lies. COUNTA, COUNT, IFERROR, SUMIFS — that is the whole dashboard grammar.

Interview line. “Nothing on the dashboard is typed. If the extract grows, the cards move.”

Before leaving the gym

From memory, on a blank sheet: date subtraction that blanks Farah, a share with $, COUNTIFS for LinkedIn joiners, XLOOKUP with “No role”, TRIM plus a map, FILTER of Outcome = Open, and three rates wrapped against zero. Then open the Ferra project chapter and do it at plant scale.
