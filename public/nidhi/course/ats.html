Chapter 4

ATS, Workday and the data behind them

Job adverts in Europe name specific systems — Workday, SuccessFactors, Greenhouse, SmartRecruiters — and you will not have used most of them. That is fine and normal. What is not fine is being unable to talk about what an ATS is, because the concepts transfer completely and the vocabulary is learnable in an afternoon.

The honesty rule for this chapter. You must not write "Workday" in your professional experience unless you have used it at work. What you can truthfully say is: "I have used [whatever you actually uses]. I have not used Workday, but I understand the model — requisitions, candidate records, stages, dispositions — and I have read the Workday Recruiting documentation. I would expect to be productive in it inside two weeks." That answer is strong. An inflated claim that collapses in week one is not.

One model, many brand names

What an ATS actually is

Every applicant tracking system, without exception, is the same four things. Learn these once and every system is a skin over them.

ObjectWhat it holdsIn your Excel tracker
RequisitionAn approved open job. Has an ID, a title, a hiring manager, a location, a headcount, an approval chain and a status.The Roles sheet.
CandidateA person. One record, which may be attached to several requisitions over time.A person's name in Candidates.
ApplicationThe link between a candidate and a requisition. This is the row that actually matters — the same person applying to two roles is two applications.One row of the Candidates sheet.
StageWhere an application has got to, and when it got there. The stage history is what makes time-in-stage reporting possible.The date columns E to J.

The insight that makes your sound experienced: the unit of recruiting data is the application, not the candidate. Count candidates and the same strong person applying to three roles looks like three people. Count applications and the funnel is correct but a headcount of "unique people in our pipeline" needs a DISTINCTCOUNT. Knowing which question needs which is exactly the sort of thing that separates someone who has reported on an ATS from someone who has only used one.

What to actually do about this. Pick one and get hands on it. Greenhouse and SmartRecruiters both publish extensive public documentation and help centres; reading a product's reporting documentation for two hours teaches the vocabulary properly. Several vendors offer free trials or demo environments. Two hours of reading plus an afternoon in a trial is enough to talk fluently, and it is honest, because you are describing what you have read and tried rather than claiming production experience.

Why your reports will be wrong

Data hygiene, and dispositions

An ATS report is only as good as what recruiters typed. This is the single biggest reason recruiting analytics is hard, and being able to name the failure modes is a strong interview signal.

FailureEffect on the reportFix
Stages skippedA candidate dragged straight from Applied to Offer has no screen or interview date, so the funnel loses them and conversion rates are wrong.Make the stage progression a required workflow, not a drag-and-drop convenience.
Rejection reasons left blankNo way to answer why candidates fail. The most valuable field in the system, routinely empty.Make the disposition reason mandatory on rejection. It is one dropdown.
Free-text source"LinkedIn", "linkedin", "LI", "Linkdin" become four sources, and source-of-hire reporting is meaningless.A closed dropdown. Never a text box.
Candidates never dispositionedHundreds of applications sit at Applied forever. Time-in-stage averages are destroyed and, in the EU, this is also a GDPR retention problem.An automated nudge, and a monthly sweep.
Duplicate candidate recordsThe same person as three records. Unique-candidate counts are wrong and the candidate gets three rejection emails.Deduplicate on email, never on name.
Offer dates vs offer-accepted datesConflating them makes acceptance rate uninterpretable.Two separate fields, and know which one the report is using.

Disposition is the ATS word for closing an application with a reason: rejected at screen, withdrew, offer declined, position cancelled. The reason codes are the richest data in the system and are almost always underused.

The answer to "how would you improve our recruiting data?" \u2014 and it should not be about dashboards. "Before building anything I would audit three fields: is source a closed dropdown, is a rejection reason mandatory, and is every application dispositioned within thirty days. Those three control whether any report afterwards is trustworthy. A dashboard on bad data is worse than no dashboard, because people believe it."

Find the damage

Getting data out

Reporting from an ATS

  1. Built-in reports. Every system ships a funnel, a time-to-fill and a source report. Fine for a quick answer, inflexible, and rarely what a manager asked for.
  2. Custom report builder. Pick an object (applications), pick fields, add filters, group. This is where most of your ATS work will happen. Workday's version is powerful and idiosyncratic; Greenhouse's is straightforward.
  3. Export to CSV, then Excel or Power BI. What you will do most. The export is the input to everything in chapters 1 and 2.
  4. Scheduled export. The system drops a file somewhere weekly and Power Query picks it up. This is the point at which your monthly report stops costing your a morning.
  5. API or data warehouse. Someone technical pipes the ATS into a database and you query it. Worth knowing the phrase; not your job to build it.

The export trap. Most ATS exports give one row per application with the current stage, not the stage history. With only the current stage you can report the funnel today and cannot report time in stage at all. If a system offers a stage-history or stage-transition export, that is the one to ask for. Knowing to ask is itself the expertise.

Non-negotiable in Europe

GDPR as it applies to recruiting

Candidate data is personal data. In the EU that is not a policy preference, it is law with real fines. You do not need to be a lawyer; you need to know the handful of things that come up weekly and never guess about the rest.

PrincipleIn practice, for a recruiter
Lawful basisProcessing needs a reason. For applicants it is usually legitimate interest or pre-contractual necessity; for keeping a CV in a talent pool after a rejection it is consent, which must be asked for explicitly and can be withdrawn.
Data minimisationOnly collect what is needed to assess the person. Date of birth, marital status and a photograph are not needed, and in several countries asking is itself a problem.
Retention limitsCandidate data cannot be kept forever. Typical policies are 6 to 24 months after a decision, varying by country. There must be a policy and it must actually be run.
Right of accessA candidate can ask for everything held about them — including interview notes. This is the one that changes behaviour: write notes that are evidence-based and job-relevant, because they may be read by the candidate.
Right to erasureA candidate can ask to be deleted, and the request must be actioned across every system and spreadsheet. Which is precisely why candidate data should not live in personal files.
Special category dataHealth, ethnicity, religion, trade union membership, sexual orientation: Article 9, far stricter. Diversity data is collected voluntarily, anonymously, aggregated, and kept entirely separate from the hiring decision.
Automated decisionsA candidate has the right not to be subject to a solely automated decision with significant effect. An AI tool that auto-rejects without human review is legally dangerous. A human must make the decision.
Transfers outside the EUSending candidate data to a non-EU system needs a legal basis. Relevant to any US-headquartered tool \u2014 and to pasting a CV into a US AI service.

The rule for this whole course, repeated because it matters most here. Never put a real candidate's name, CV, phone number, pay details or interview notes into a practice file, a portfolio, a public AI tool, or anything you keep personally. Not anonymised. Not "just for practice". Every exercise in this course uses six invented people for exactly this reason, and saying so in an interview is a point in your favour, not an admission.

The interview answer

Talking about a system you have not used

Before moving on

You should be able to draw the four ATS objects on a whiteboard, explain why the application rather than the candidate is the unit of data, name three European ATS products, list three data-hygiene fields you would audit first, and state the GDPR rules on retention, access to interview notes and special category data.
