Chapter 1.2

Logic and counting

This chapter builds the recruiting funnel. By the end of it you will have counted six stages, calculated three rates, and be able to say what each number means and what you would do about it. That block of numbers is the thing an interviewer actually asks to see.

The workhorse

IF, and why every recruiting formula needs one

The shape is always the same three parts:

=IF( something that is true or false , what to show if true , what to show if false )

In recruiting, the test is almost always did this stage happen yet?, because a pipeline is mostly made of things that have not happened. Without the test, a duration against an empty cell returns a meaningless number in the tens of thousands:

FormulaFarah, who has no Accepted date
=I7-E7-46106 — you subtracted a real date from zero
=IF(I7="","",I7-E7)blank, which is the honest answer

"" means an empty piece of text. Reading it as "nothing" is close enough. Two notes that matter later: a cell holding "" looks empty but COUNTA counts it as filled, and it is not the same as a genuinely empty cell. That is fine here, because the thing reading this column will be COUNT, which only counts numbers.

Nesting, and when to stop

IF can hold another IF in its false branch. Two deep is readable. Four deep is a formula nobody — including your, in March — can debug.

=IF(N2<=20,"Fast",IF(N2<=35,"Normal","Slow"))

IFS does the same thing without the nesting. It takes pairs — test, result, test, result — and the first test that is true wins, so order matters. TRUE as the last test is the catch-all:

=IFS(N2<=20,"Fast", N2<=35,"Normal", TRUE,"Slow")

Prefer IFS from three branches up. And if there are more than four, the right answer is not a formula at all — it is a small lookup table and an XLOOKUP, which is chapter 1.3.

Two conditions at once

AND, OR, NOT

These do not display anything useful on their own — they return TRUE or FALSE, and live inside an IF.

  • AND(…) is TRUE only when every test is true. "Still open and older than fourteen days."
  • OR(…) is TRUE when at least one is. "Declined or reneged" — the two ways a win becomes a loss.
  • NOT(…) flips it. Mostly used as NOT(ISBLANK(x)), meaning "this stage did happen".
  • XOR(…) is TRUE when exactly one is. It exists. You will never need it. Recognise it and move on.

The one that will actually earn its keep every Monday morning is an ageing flag:

=IF(AND([@Outcome]="Open", TODAY()-[@Applied]>14), "CHASE", "")

One column, filled down the whole table, and your Monday list writes itself. In the practice data only Farah lights up — applied on 20 March, never screened, and this course's clock says it is 1 May. Six weeks. That is the single most useful formula in this chapter, because it turns a spreadsheet from a record of the past into a to-do list.

A subtlety worth knowing. AND inside a single cell works as expected. People sometimes expect AND to work across a whole column at once — =AND(Candidates[Outcome]="Open") — which collapses the column into one TRUE/FALSE and is almost never what was meant. To test every row and count the hits, use COUNTIFS, which is lesson 4.

Rates that do not go red

IFERROR, and dividing safely

Every rate is a division, and every division can meet a zero. A role with no offers yet has an acceptance rate that is not zero — it is undefined, and showing 0% would be a lie, because 0% means "we made offers and everyone said no".

=IFERROR( accepts / offers , "" )

Blank is the honest display. There are three tools and they are not interchangeable:

ToolCatchesUse when
IFERROR(x, alt)Every errorDivisions. The error is expected and means "not applicable yet".
IFNA(x, alt)Only #N/ALookups, when a missing key is acceptable — but you still wants to see a #VALUE! if the formula itself is broken. Safer than IFERROR.
IF(offers=0,"",accepts/offers)Nothing — it avoids the errorWhen you want to be explicit about the one case you are handling. Longer, clearer, and it cannot accidentally hide a different bug.

The rule from chapter 1.1, restated because it matters: wrap divisions, investigate lookups. An #N/A buried under an IFERROR is a broken record that nobody will ever find.

The funnel itself

The COUNT family

Five functions, and choosing the wrong one is how two reports of the same pipeline disagree.

FunctionCountsOn the practice data
COUNTNumbers and dates only. Ignores text and blanks.=COUNT(Candidates[Offer]) → 4. This is the one that counts a funnel stage.
COUNTAAnything not empty, including text.=COUNTA(Candidates[Name]) → 6. Use it for "how many rows".
COUNTBLANKEmpty cells.=COUNTBLANK(Candidates[Joined]) → 4. Four of six have not started.
COUNTIFRows meeting one condition.=COUNTIF(Candidates[Source],"LinkedIn") → 4
COUNTIFSRows meeting several conditions, all of them.=COUNTIFS(Candidates[Source],"LinkedIn",Candidates[Outcome],"Joined") → 2

Why COUNT and not COUNTA for a stage. If one cell in the Offer column contains the word "pending" instead of a date, COUNTA counts it and COUNT does not. COUNT is asking "on how many dated occasions did this happen", which is the real question. COUNTA is asking "how many cells have something in them", which is a question about the spreadsheet, not about the hiring.

Criteria, written properly

The criteria argument is a small language, and it goes in quotes:

CriteriaMeans
"LinkedIn"Exactly that. Case-insensitive, so "linkedin" also matches.
">30"Greater than 30. The operator goes inside the quotes.
">="&TODAY()-30Within the last thirty days. Note the & — an operator in quotes joined to a live value.
"<>"Not blank.
""Blank.
"Link*"Starts with "Link". * is any number of characters, ? is exactly one.

That third row is worth re-reading. ">="&TODAY()-30 is how a rolling window is written, and a rolling window — "applications in the last thirty days" — is most of what a hiring manager asks for.

Money and totals

SUM, SUMIF, SUMIFS

Identical thinking to the COUNT family, with one trap: in SUMIFS the column being added comes first, while in SUMIF it comes last. That inconsistency is Excel's fault and it catches everybody.

=SUMIF( range to test , criteria , range to add ) =SUMIFS( range to add , range to test , criteria , … )

Use SUMIFS always, even for a single condition. One habit, one argument order, and it extends to a second condition without rewriting.

The number this produces in your file is agency spend, and it is the most politically useful number you will own:

=SUMIFS(Candidates[FeeEUR], Candidates[Source], "Agency") → 4000

€4,000, on Eva, who accepted and never started. The fee is owed regardless. When you say in an interview "I reduced agency dependency", this is the number that made the argument — not a sentiment.

And the honest version of it

Averages, and what an average hides

AVERAGE ignores blanks and text, which is usually helpful and occasionally a trap. Average time to hire across the three people who accepted is 31.3 days. Across all six candidates it is meaningless, because three of them have not got there.

AVERAGEIFS restricts it properly, and takes the same argument order as SUMIFS — the column to average first:

=AVERAGEIFS(Candidates[HireDays], Candidates[Outcome], "Joined") → 27.5

27.5 days for the two who actually started, against 31.3 for everyone who accepted. The difference is Eva, who took 39 days to accept and then never arrived. Two defensible numbers, two different stories. Which one you quote is a choice you have to be able to defend, and an interviewer who knows the field will ask.

The four summaries, and when each lies

FunctionGivesLies when
AVERAGEThe mean, 31.3One extreme value drags it. One 90-day aerospace hire moves the average of ten roles by nine days.
MEDIANThe middle value, 32Rarely. With small numbers of hires, the median is usually the more honest headline.
MAXThe worst case, 39Never lies, and is the number the hiring manager remembers.
MINThe best case, 23When quoted alone. A recruiter quoting only their fastest hire is telling on themselves.

Report the median and the max together. "Median 32 days, worst case 39" is one sentence, cannot be argued with, and is far more useful than a mean. MODE returns the most frequent value and is almost useless on durations — keep it for "which source appears most", where COUNTIFS is better anyway.

The capstone

The rates, and the sentence that goes with them

Counts tell your what happened. Rates tell your where to act. Three of them matter and they are frequently confused — including by interviewers, which is an opportunity.

RateFormulaHereWhat a bad one means
Offer acceptanceaccepts ÷ offers3 / 4 = 75%Pay is wrong, the process was slow, or the manager undersold the role at the final stage.
Joining ratejoiners ÷ accepts2 / 3 = 67%A renege. Usually a counter-offer during a long notice period, and the fix is contact during notice, not better interviewing.
Screen-to-interviewinterviews ÷ screened4 / 5 = 80%If it is low, you are sending the wrong people — a sourcing problem, not a manager problem.

The distinction between the first two is the single most common thing recruiters get wrong, and it is worth being precise about: acceptance is about the offer, joining is about everything after it. A team with 95% acceptance and 70% joining does not have an offer problem. It has a notice-period problem, and the fix is a completely different conversation.

"Acceptance is 75 percent, but one of the three who accepted never started, so our joining rate is 67. The renege was the agency candidate, after a six-week notice period, and we paid the €4,000 anyway. I'd rather fix the gap between accept and start than brief another agency role."

That is the paragraph the whole chapter exists to produce. Numbers, then the cause, then the decision.

Reading someone else's file

Auditing a formula you did not write

You will inherit spreadsheets. Often from someone who has left. Four moves, in order:

  1. Press Ctrl+` to show every formula instead of its result. The shape of the file becomes visible immediately — including the cell where someone typed a number over a formula. Try the Show formulas button on any grid in this course.
  2. Look for typed constants in a calculated column. One hard-coded 47 in a column of formulas is the usual reason a total is wrong, and it is invisible until this step.
  3. Check the ranges reach the bottom. A SUM over B2:B99 in a sheet with 400 rows is the classic. Tables prevent this, which is why 1.1 lesson 6 exists.
  4. Trace Precedents — Formulas > Trace Precedents — draws arrows to every cell feeding the selected one. The fastest way to find a link to a workbook on someone's old laptop.

What to say when you find a mistake in a report someone else has been sending for a year. Not "this is wrong". Try: "I rebuilt the time-to-fill calculation and I get a different number — can I show you the two and check which definition we want?" The number is probably wrong. The relationship is worth more than being right loudly.
