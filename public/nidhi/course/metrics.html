Chapter 3

Recruiting metrics

Excel and Power BI are how you calculate. This chapter is what to calculate and, more importantly, what each number is for. The difference between a recruiter and a recruiting analyst is that the analyst knows which metric answers which question — and which metric is being misused.

The shape of every hiring process

The funnel

Every recruiting number is a count at a stage or a ratio between two stages. Once the funnel is drawn, the metrics are obvious.

The practice data as a funnel. Note the two places it narrows most: screen-to-interview, and accept-to-join.

The ratios between neighbouring stages are the diagnostic. A single low ratio tells your where the process is failing, and each one has a different owner:

RatioIf it is low, the problem isWhose problem
Applicant → screenThe advert is attracting the wrong people, or the screening bar is wrongHers — sourcing and the job advert
Screen → interviewYou and the hiring manager disagree about what "good" meansA calibration conversation, not a sourcing problem
Interview → offerThe interview is finding problems a screen should have caught, or the bar movedShared — usually a scorecard problem
Offer → acceptPay, the competition, or how the candidate was treatedCompensation and the manager's selling
Accept → joinA long notice period and nobody staying in touchHers — onboarding contact between accept and start

The sentence that makes this a strategic answer. "I look at the conversion between each pair of stages rather than the overall rate, because the overall rate tells me something is wrong and the stage ratio tells me what. In this data the screen-to-interview rate is 80% but the accept-to-join rate is 67% — so the problem is not sourcing, it is the gap between accepting and starting."

The most-quoted and most-misused metric

Time to hire, time to fill, time in stage

Three different numbers that get called "time to hire" interchangeably. Knowing the difference is a credibility marker.

MetricMeasured from → toWhat it is for
Time to hireCandidate applied → candidate acceptedHow fast the process moves once someone is in it. This is the recruiter's efficiency metric.
Time to fillRequisition opened → candidate acceptedHow long the business waits. Includes the time spent finding anyone at all. This is the number a hiring manager cares about.
Time to start / productivityRequisition opened → first dayIncludes notice periods. The only one that reflects when work actually gets done.
Time in stageEntered a stage → left itThe useful one. It localises the delay instead of averaging it away.

Why you should quote the median, not the mean. One role that took 180 days drags the average up and makes a healthy process look broken. The median is the typical experience. The professional answer is to report both: "median 31 days, mean 44 — the gap is one Berlin role that sat open for four months while we waited on a visa, and I would exclude it from the trend."

Identifying and explaining the outlier, rather than hiding it or letting it distort the headline, is the behaviour being assessed.

The hard one, and the one that matters

Quality of hire

Speed and cost are easy to measure and almost beside the point. A fast, cheap, wrong hire is the most expensive thing a recruiting function can produce. Quality of hire is hard because it is only knowable months later — and that is exactly why most teams do not measure it, which is the opportunity.

ProxyHowHonest limitation
Passed probationPercentage still employed after 3 or 6 monthsA low bar. It catches disasters, not mediocrity.
First-year retentionPercentage still there at 12 monthsThe standard. Separate voluntary from involuntary leavers — they mean opposite things.
Hiring manager satisfactionOne question at 90 days: "would you hire this person again?"Subjective, but cheap and surprisingly predictive. The trend across many hires is what matters, not one score.
Time to productivityWeeks until the manager says the person is fully contributingNeeds a definition per role or it is noise.
Performance rating at first reviewCompare to the company distributionRatings are inconsistent between managers, so compare within a team, not across.
Early attritionPercentage leaving within 6 monthsThe sharpest signal. A spike here is a recruiting or onboarding failure, not an employee failure.

The composite worth proposing. One simple index: passed probation (yes/no), manager would rehire (yes/no), still here at 12 months (yes/no). Three booleans, scored out of three, averaged across a cohort. It is crude and defensible, it can be collected with a two-question form, and proposing it in an interview demonstrates you think past the metrics that are easy to count.

The line to have ready: "Most teams measure how fast and how cheap, because that data is already in the ATS. Almost nobody measures whether the hire was any good, because that data has to be collected on purpose. I would rather be accountable for the second one."

What finance asks about

Cost per hire, and agency spend

The standard definition, from the SHRM/ANSI standard, is worth knowing precisely because people quote it loosely:

Cost per hire = ( internal costs + external costs ) ÷ number of hires
  • External: agency fees, job board subscriptions, LinkedIn Recruiter licences, advertising, careers-event costs, assessment tools, background checks, relocation.
  • Internal: the recruiting team's salaries, referral bonuses, the share of the ATS cost, and — the one everyone forgets — interviewer time.

Interviewer time is the honest one. Four engineers at an hour each, plus prep and debrief, on a salary of €90,000, is roughly €250 of real cost per candidate interviewed. Interview twelve people for one role and that is €3,000 of engineering time — frequently more than the advertising budget, and entirely invisible in most cost-per-hire figures.

Cost per hire alone is a dangerous metric, because the cheapest way to improve it is to stop investing in hiring. Always pair it with quality and time: "cost per hire fell 30% because we moved two roles off agencies, and first-year retention held at 92%." That is a defensible claim. "Cost per hire fell 30%" on its own invites the question of what was sacrificed.

The agency decision, with real numbers

A 20% agency fee on a €70,000 role is €14,000. Change the inputs and see when an agency is actually the rational choice.

The answer that shows judgement. Agencies are not evil and not always wrong. They are right when the role is genuinely hard to source, when it is urgent enough that the cost of the vacancy exceeds the fee, or when you have no network in that market. They are wrong as a default, and as a substitute for building a pipeline. Saying "I would use an agency for the first hire in a new country and build direct sourcing behind it" is a far better answer than either extreme.

Where good people actually come from

Source effectiveness

The mistake is counting applicants by source. Volume is not value. Count joiners by source, then compare the two.

On the practice data: LinkedIn is 4 of 6 applicants (67%) and 1 of 2 joiners (50%). Referral is 1 of 6 applicants (17%) and 1 of 2 joiners (50%). Referrals produce half the hires from a sixth of the volume — which, if it held over a larger sample, is the argument for a referral scheme.

MetricDefinitionWhat it answers
Source mixApplicants by source, as a percentageWhere the volume comes from. Weakest of the three.
Source of hireJoiners by sourceWhere the results come from. This is the one to report.
Source qualityJoiners ÷ applicants, per sourceWhich channel converts. A channel with 5% of applicants and 30% of hires deserves investment.
Cost per sourceSpend ÷ joiners, per sourceWhich channel is worth the money. Referrals usually win and nobody budgets for them.
Applicant-to-interview per sourceInterviews ÷ applicants, per sourceWhich channel wastes your screening time. A job board producing 200 applicants and 2 interviews is a cost, not a source.

Carefully, and legally

Diversity metrics in Europe

This is a genuine area of expertise and a genuine legal minefield, and the two are related. What matters is knowing the boundary.

MeasureStatus in the EU
Gender, aggregatedGenerally collectable and widely reported. The EU Pay Transparency Directive requires gender pay gap reporting, with obligations phasing in from June 2026.
EthnicityVaries enormously. Routine in the UK and Ireland; in France collecting ethnic statistics is essentially prohibited; Germany is very restrictive. Never assume a UK approach transfers.
DisabilitySpecial category data under GDPR Article 9. Voluntary, anonymous, aggregated, and strictly separate from a hiring decision.
AgeUsually derivable and usually should not be. Do not put date of birth in a tracker.

The three rules that keep your safe and credible.

  1. Aggregate only, never individual. A diversity report says "34% of applicants, 21% of hires". It never says which candidate was which.
  2. Measure the funnel, not just the intake. This is the insight most people miss. If a group is 40% of applicants and 15% of hires, the problem is not sourcing — it is somewhere in the screening or interviewing, and the stage ratios will show where. Reporting intake alone hides the actual bias.
  3. Suppress small numbers. Below about five people a percentage identifies individuals. The convention is not to report cells under 5. Knowing this unprompted signals real experience with sensitive data.

Never build a diversity report in a personal file with real candidate data in it. Not anonymised, not "just for practice". This is special category data under GDPR. Practise the technique on the invented data in this course, and talk about the approach in interviews. The numbers in a portfolio must be fictional.

What to actually put on the dashboard

The monthly scorecard

Not every metric that can be calculated should be reported. A scorecard that a hiring manager reads has about eight numbers, each with a comparison and, where it matters, a sentence.

NumberShown asWhy it is on the page
Open requisitionsCount, split by stageThe workload, at a glance.
Joiners this monthCount vs planThe output. Against plan, or it is just a number.
Median time to fillDays, vs last quarterTrend matters; the absolute figure means nothing without one.
Offer acceptancePercentage, vs last quarterAn early warning about pay and candidate experience.
Source of hireStacked barJustifies next year's budget.
Agency spend€, vs budgetThe number finance will ask about first.
Roles open > 60 daysA named listPrompts action instead of describing history. The most valuable thing on the page.
First-year retention of last year's hiresPercentageThe only quality signal, and the one that keeps the rest honest.

Every number gets a comparison. "Time to fill: 38 days" is unreadable. "Time to fill: 38 days, up from 31 last quarter, driven by two Berlin roles waiting on visas" is a report. The comparison and the cause are the work; the number is just the prompt.

Before moving on

You should be able to define time to hire, time to fill, cost per hire and quality of hire without hesitating, explain why the median beats the mean on time to fill, say which stage ratio points at which owner, and name the small-numbers suppression rule for diversity data.
