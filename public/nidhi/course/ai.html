Chapter 9

AI drafts. You decide.

Every European talent team is being asked what it is doing with generative AI. Very few recruiters can answer carefully. The careful answer is a short list of jobs the tool is good at, a hard list of jobs it must not do, and one certificate taken after your own files exist. This chapter is that answer, practised.

Six jobs it is actually good at

Where it helps

The tool is a fast intern who has never met the hiring manager and has never seen the search results. You give it structure. You keep the facts. You throw away anything it invented.

JobWhat you paste inWhat you do with the output
A job descriptionThe intake notes: mandatory skills, false matches, city, three true reasons to joinReads it against what Maya actually said. Deletes benefits the company does not offer. Shortens the mandatory list.
A Boolean draftThe role, the city, the stack, the people to excludeAsks for five strings from narrow to wide. Runs them. Keeps the one that returns the right people. The tool has not seen the results.
Screen questionsThe mandatory skills only — never the candidate's CVUses them as a prompt for your own conversation, not as a script you read.
A hiring-manager summaryNotes you took on the callStructure: relevant work, gap, pay, notice, location, recommendation. If a sentence was not in the CV or the call, it comes out.
Market homeworkThe role and the cityAsks for the kinds of companies that employ this role, then checks they exist. Invented employers are common.
Your own EnglishA message you already wroteTakes the tighter version only if it still sounds like you. You send that one.

The prompt that actually works is boring: "Here are the intake notes. Write a job description of 250 words. Mandatory skills only, no more than five. Do not invent benefits. End with a published salary range of €X–€Y. Flag anything you made up." Specific, bounded, and it asks the model to confess.

Do the work once, by hand, so you can judge the machine

Then compare with a model. The point is not to sound like a copywriter. The point is to practise the judgement you will use on anything a model produces.

What Maya forwarded

"We are looking for a passionate Backend Engineer to join our fast-paced, family-like culture in Dublin. Must have 5+ years Java, Spring, Kafka, Kubernetes, Terraform, AWS, microservices, and excellent communication. Competitive salary. Great benefits. Be a self-starter who thrives in ambiguity and wears many hats. Apply now with CV and expected salary."

What you actually said in the intake

They will own the payments API. Must: Java or Kotlin, SQL, production on-call. Should: Kafka. Nice: Kubernetes. Last good hire had four years, not five, and had never used Terraform. Band €72–80k. Can sponsor if it clears €68,911. Hybrid, three days in Dublin. They will work with Maya and two other backend engineers. Do not say family-like — the last person who left said the hours were the problem.

The list that keeps your employable

Where it stops

Four hard rules. They are not personal ethics; they are GDPR, discrimination law, and the thing that gets a recruiter fired.

No real candidate data in a public tool. Not a CV, not a phone number, not a salary, not interview notes, not "anonymised" if you would still recognise them. Practice data stays fictional, like Asha and Ben. A company tool with a data-processing agreement is a different conversation, and even then you ask before pasting.

No automatic rejection. A model repeats the bias in the histories it saw. A human owns the decision, and you must be able to explain it. If you cannot say why someone was screened out, the tool does not get to be the reason.

No score the candidate cannot understand. "The AI scored you 41" is not a reason. "You have not shipped a production service with on-call" is a reason. The second one also survives a subject-access request.

No invented facts, about candidates or about you. The model will fabricate employers, metrics and certifications. You check every claim against the CV, the call, or your own tracker. The same rule as the rest of this plan.

The answer they are actually testing

What to say when they ask about AI

Interviewers are not looking for a prompt library. They are looking for someone who will not leak a candidate's data and will not hide behind a score. Forty-five seconds, then compare.

The one certificate that earns its line

IBM's course, and the one to take in pieces

Two Coursera specialisations were considered. Only one belongs on the CV in these four months.

What it isThree courses, about 29 hours. Models, applying them to HR work, and the responsible-use side.Five courses, around six months at four hours a week. Recruiting, performance, compensation, a capstone.

The catchUseless if you cannot point at a tracker and a report of your own.US employment law throughout — at-will, FLSA, EEOC. Quoting that in Dublin will land badly.

The free path still does more of the job than either paid course: W3Schools and GeeksforGeeks for Excel, Microsoft Learn for Power BI. PL-300 waits until after you are hired. A LinkedIn Recruiter course you cannot practise on is a waste of money. Any certificate in prompting is a waste of a line.

What "done" looks like for IBM. You finish the three courses. You rewrite one job description from your own intake notes using the method it taught, and you keep both versions. In an interview you can say what you changed and why. That is the portfolio, not the badge.

Before moving on

You should be able to name six uses, four hard stops, and the sentence for the interview. You should have one rewritten JD in your own words. Extra reps: craft and job labs. You should not have put a real CV into anything.
