Chapter 5

Sourcing and Boolean search

Analytics gets your shortlisted; sourcing is what you do on the job. It is also the most demonstrable skill in recruiting — a search string either returns the right people or it does not, and that can be shown in an interview in sixty seconds.

Four operators, that is all

Boolean, properly

Boolean search is the same logic as AND and OR in Excel, which you already knows. Four operators and two pieces of punctuation.

OperatorEffectExample
ANDBoth must appear. Narrows.python AND dublin
OREither will do. Widens. Always bracket an OR group.(python OR django)
NOTExcludes. Use sparingly — it is the operator that loses good people.NOT recruiter
" "An exact phrase."backend engineer"
( )Grouping. Without it the engine's precedence decides, and it will not decide what you meant.(python OR go) AND dublin
*Wildcard on some engines, not on LinkedIn. Use OR instead.recruit*

The bracket mistake, which everybody makes once.

python OR java AND dublin does not mean what it looks like. AND binds tighter than OR, so it reads as python, OR (java in Dublin) — returning every Python developer on the planet. The fix is one pair of brackets: (python OR java) AND dublin.

The anatomy of a good string

Four blocks, joined by AND, each block an OR group:

("backend engineer" OR "backend developer" OR "software engineer") AND (python OR django OR flask) AND (dublin OR "co. dublin" OR leinster) AND NOT (recruiter OR "talent acquisition" OR student OR intern)

Read it as four questions: what do they do, what do they know, where are they, and who do I definitely not want. Every good string is that shape, and the fourth block is the one people forget — a search for recruiting terms without it returns mostly other recruiters.

Build one and see who comes back

The search simulator

Twelve invented profiles. The brief: a backend engineer in Dublin who writes Python. Four of the twelve are worth calling. Click terms to build a string and watch which profiles survive.

Start with just a job title and see how wide it is. Then narrow. The counter underneath tells your whether you have gone too far.

What "good" looks like. A clean string returns the people worth calling and few others. A string that returns everyone is a job-title search and has done no work. A string that returns three perfect people and excludes a fourth good one is worse than a slightly wide one — because you will never know who you missed. When in doubt, be a little too wide and skim.

That asymmetry is the key judgement in sourcing: a false positive costs your thirty seconds, a false negative costs your a candidate. It is why NOT should be used for obvious noise — recruiters, students — and never for anything subtle.

The X-ray string, since it is free and you should be able to type it from memory:

site:linkedin.com/in ("backend engineer" OR "software engineer") python (dublin OR ireland) -recruiter

Note Google uses - rather than NOT, and site: restricts to one domain. Being able to write this unprompted in an interview is a concrete, checkable demonstration of sourcing skill — far better than saying you are "good at sourcing".

The part that decides whether it works

Outreach that gets replies

A perfect search with generic outreach produces nothing. Response rates are the measurable output, and the difference between a 5% and a 30% reply rate is entirely in the message.

What gets ignored

"Hi, I came across your profile and was impressed by your background. I'm recruiting for an exciting opportunity with a market-leading client. Are you open to new opportunities? Let me know a good time to chat."

No specifics, no role, no company, no reason it is them. It is obviously sent to four hundred people, because it was.

What gets replies

"Hi Róisín — I saw your talk at PyCon Ireland on migrating a monolith to services. We're hiring a backend engineer at [company] in Dublin doing exactly that migration, Python and Postgres, €75–90k, hybrid two days. Worth a twenty-minute call? If the timing is wrong, no problem — happy to send the spec for later."

One specific reason it is them, the actual role, the actual pay, the actual location, a small ask, and an easy exit.

ElementWhy it matters
One specific detail about themProves a human read the profile. One line is enough, and it must be real — a fabricated compliment is worse than none.
The actual company name"A market-leading client" means an agency fishing. Naming the company is a trust signal.
The salary rangeThe highest-leverage line in the message. It saves everyone's time, and from June 2026 the EU Pay Transparency Directive makes it an obligation rather than a courtesy.
Location and working patternRemote, hybrid or onsite is a dealbreaker for many people and takes six words.
A small, specific ask"Twenty minutes" is answerable. "Let me know a good time to chat" is a task.
An easy noPermission to decline raises reply rates, and a polite no today is a warm contact next year.
ShortUnder 120 words. It will be read on a phone.

Where this joins chapter 3

Measuring sourcing

This is the bridge between the sourcing skill and the analytics skill, and it is what makes your unusual: most sourcers do not measure, and most analysts cannot source.

MetricDefinitionA reasonable figure
Response rateReplies ÷ messages sentGeneric outreach: 5–15%. Well-targeted and specific: 25–40%. The gap is the message, not the list.
Positive response rateInterested replies ÷ messagesThe one that matters. A 40% reply rate that is all polite declines is a targeting problem.
Sourced → screenReached screening ÷ repliedTests whether the string found the right people or merely responsive ones.
Sourced → hireHires ÷ messages sentOften 1–2%, which is why volume matters — but improving the message beats sending more.
Time to first responseDays from message to replyTells your when to follow up. Most replies arrive in 48 hours; one follow-up after a week is worth sending, two is not.

Work out whether a change helped

The portfolio piece for this chapter. A one-page sourcing report on an invented search: the brief, the string you used, how many profiles it returned, how many you contacted, the reply rate, and one sentence on what you would change next time. It is small, it is entirely fictional, and it demonstrates both skills at once. Almost nobody brings one.

The European constraints

Sourcing legally in Europe

  • A public profile is not consent. Looking is fine. Storing someone's details in a talent pool needs a lawful basis, and after a first contact that basis is usually consent. In practice: contact, and only add them to a pool if they say yes.
  • Tell them where you found them if asked. GDPR gives people the right to know the source of their data, and "I found your talk at PyCon" is a better opening line anyway.
  • No scraping. Bulk-harvesting LinkedIn profiles into a spreadsheet breaches LinkedIn's terms and creates a database with no lawful basis. It is also the fastest way to lose an account.
  • Never store a CV or contact details in a personal file. This is the course rule, and it applies with full force here. Your sourcing notes live in the company's ATS, not in your own spreadsheet.
  • Pay transparency is arriving. The EU Pay Transparency Directive must be in national law by June 2026 and requires pay information to candidates, with a ban on asking about salary history. Mentioning that you already include ranges in outreach shows you are ahead of it.
  • No discriminatory filters. Filtering by age, nationality, gender or a photograph is unlawful in the EU. "Native speaker" in an advert is a well-known trap — the lawful phrasing is a required proficiency level such as C1.

Before moving on

You should be able to write a four-block Boolean string from memory, write a Google X-ray string from memory, explain why AND binds tighter than OR, draft a 100-word outreach message with all seven elements, quote a realistic response rate, and name the GDPR constraint on talent pools.
