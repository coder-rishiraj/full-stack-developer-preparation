Money and permission to work

Ask for the visa floor, not a modest salary.

All figures below are gross, before tax. A normal market salary can still be too low for the visa. That is the whole game for a non-EU candidate.

First choice

Ireland · Dublin

€70,000

Morgan McKinley’s 2026 guide puts a Dublin Talent Acquisition Partner with 5+ years at €60,000–€70,000. Three to five years sits at €55,000–€60,000. Nidhi is at the boundary: long enough on paper, but Amazon was not recruiting and the specialised engineering work is recent. Quote €65,000–€75,000. The number that matters for immigration is €68,911.

  • Talent acquisition is not on Ireland’s Critical Skills occupations list. HR administrative occupations are on the ineligible list. The offer must be a professional recruiting role, not “HR admin.”
  • At €68,911 or more, a role that is not ineligible can use a Critical Skills Employment Permit: no labour-market test, and a faster route to Stamp 4. The job offer needs to be for two years.
  • Below that, the route is usually a General Employment Permit. The 2026 minimum is €36,605, but the employer must usually run a labour-market test and show no suitable EEA candidate. That is slower and easier for the company to abandon.
  • A €60,000 offer can be a fair market salary and still a weak visa. Prefer €70,000 when the company will sponsor.
Second choice

Netherlands · Amsterdam

€71,300

Market pay for a TA Partner is often around €60,000–€75,000. The IND highly skilled migrant rule for 2026 is stricter, and the employer must be a recognised sponsor.

  • Age 30 or over: €5,942 gross per month, excluding the 8% holiday allowance. That is about €71,304 a year before holiday pay. Holiday pay is extra and does not count toward the threshold.
  • Under 30: €4,357 a month, about €52,284 a year, again excluding holiday allowance.
  • Her B.Com starts in 2013. She should check her date of birth against the day the employer files, and assume the higher band unless she is clearly under 30.
  • Do not accept a Dutch offer of €60,000 if she is 30 or older. It can be a normal salary and still fail the permit.
Third, unless she learns German

Germany

€55–70k

This is the best industrial market for her Ferra experience: Airbus, Siemens, Bosch, Rheinmetall, and the supplier network. English-only multinational roles exist. Most HR jobs want German.

  • The 2026 EU Blue Card standard threshold is €50,700 gross. The lower figure of €45,934 applies to shortage occupations, recent graduates and some IT specialists. HR is not a shortage occupation, and her MBA finished in 2021, so plan on €50,700.
  • The job has to match the qualification. An MBA in finance and marketing can support a business role, but a visa officer still has to accept that talent acquisition matches the degree. Do not treat this as automatic.
  • Market offers for TA partners are often €50,000–€65,000, with €65,000–€75,000 possible in Berlin or at a multinational. Target €60,000–€70,000 so the offer clears the Blue Card with room.
  • If Germany becomes the priority, 25 minutes of German a day starts now. B1 changes the number of jobs. Four months will not produce B2 from zero, so English-speaking employers stay the 2027 target.
Do not rely on this

United Kingdom

£45–65k

London and multinational TA roles can pay in this band, sometimes higher. Sponsorship is the problem, not the salary guide.

  • Since July 2025 the standard Skilled Worker floor is the higher of £41,700 and the job’s going rate, and most new sponsorships need a degree-level occupation.
  • If the role is classed as an HR or recruitment manager, published going-rate tables put that occupation near £52,900. A recruiter or consultant classification may not be sponsorable at all for a new applicant.
  • Apply to UK roles that state sponsorship. Do not make Britain the plan.
After the first European job

Apple, Meta, Google and the rest

Later

She can work in these companies. They employ recruiters, sourcers and talent partners. She cannot walk in next month as a senior technical recruiter for software. Meta does not need a CNC specialist.

  • First European job: Talent Acquisition Partner or Technical Recruiter at a multinational, an aerospace or industrial firm, or a technology company one tier down. Expect €60,000–€80,000 gross, with Ireland and the Netherlands pushed to the visa floors above.
  • After one to three years of European technical hiring, senior TA and big-tech packages move up because they add bonus and stock. Reported UK packages at stronger recruiter levels can pass £100,000 total. That is a destination, not the number to say in a November screen.
  • Companies worth a stretch application once month 2 is done: Google, Meta, Apple, Microsoft, Amazon, Salesforce, Stripe, Uber, HubSpot, ServiceNow, Workday, LinkedIn, Datadog, Atlassian, Booking.com, Adyen, Spotify.

What to say when they ask for expected salary

For a Talent Acquisition Partner role I’m looking at €70,000 to €80,000 gross in Ireland, or the local equivalent that meets the work-permit threshold. I’m flexible on the mix of base, bonus and relocation, and I need the offer to be sponsorable.

Never open at €50,000. Compare offers on gross pay, bonus, pension, health insurance, leave, relocation and who pays the visa — not on the headline alone. Take-home is much lower after tax.

She does not need another general HR certificate. CIPD and SHRM are real qualifications. They are the wrong use of these four months. The order is below. Open the course

01

Excel that can survive an interview

XLOOKUP, INDEX/MATCH, SUMIFS, COUNTIFS, IF/IFS, dates, PivotTables, conditional formatting, and a basic Power Query clean. Then rebuild the Ferra HR dashboard herself — Filter Engine, live cards, funnel conversions — from the course project chapter. “Advanced Excel” on a CV without a file is useless.

02

One Power BI recruiting dashboard

Power Query, a simple model, relationships, and a few measures: COUNTROWS, DISTINCTCOUNT, DIVIDE, CALCULATE. The dashboard shows the funnel, time to fill, source of hire, offer acceptance, ageing roles and hires by location. HR-analytics level. Not a data-analyst certificate.

03

What the metrics mean

Time to fill is not time to hire. Offer acceptance is not joining rate. Source of hire is not source of applicants. She should be able to say which number she would show a hiring manager, and what she would do if interview-to-offer collapses.

04

Sourcing

Boolean search, LinkedIn Recruiter concepts (projects, pools, InMail, alerts), Google X-ray, and a basic look at GitHub for engineers. Example she should be able to write without notes: ("Manufacturing Engineer" OR "Production Engineer") AND (Aerospace OR Aviation) AND (CNC OR CMM). And the software version in week 5.

05

Enough technology to recruit it

No programming. She needs to know Java is not JavaScript, React is not Node, a data analyst is not a data engineer, and AWS is not Kubernetes. Roles to recognise: frontend, backend, full stack, DevOps, SRE, data engineer, data scientist, ML engineer, product manager, engineering manager.

06

European recruiting

GDPR in hiring: purpose, minimisation, retention, access and erasure. Notice periods, probation, permanent versus contractor, right to work, and who pays for the visa. Works councils in Germany at a concept level. She is not becoming a lawyer.

07

TA partner behaviour

Workforce planning, build versus buy, permanent versus contractor, salary benchmarking, and talent mapping. The sentence to practise: “I looked at the market, reset the hiring manager’s expectations, chose the channels, sized the funnel, and watched conversion.” That replaces “the manager gave me a requirement and I sourced.”

08

AI, used carefully

Job descriptions, Boolean drafts, candidate summaries, interview questions, and market notes. She also needs the limits: biased screening, invented candidate facts, and personal data pasted into a public tool. No prompt-engineering certificate.

Workday, Greenhouse, SuccessFactors, Lever, SmartRecruiters, iCIMS. Learn the flow: requisition, posting, application, screen, interview, feedback, offer, checks, hire. Put this under training. Do not write “Workday” in professional experience unless she has used it at work.

The one course worth paying for

IBM — Generative AI for Human Resources (HR) Professionals. Three courses, about 29 hours. Take it in weeks 10 and 11, after the Excel file and the Power BI page exist. Every European talent team is being asked what it is doing with AI, and very few recruiters can answer carefully. This is the one line under Certifications that earns its place.

The one to take selectively

University of Minnesota — HR for People Managers. Five courses, six months at four hours a week — too long as a whole. Take Recruiting, Hiring and Onboarding and Managing Employee Compensation only, audited free if she likes. Warning: the employment law in it is American. The frameworks transfer, the law does not.

Everything else she needs is free: W3Schools and GeeksforGeeks for Excel, Microsoft Learn for Power BI. The course is where those topics are taught against her practice file, not as a reading list.

Leave these alone until she has a European job. Tally, payroll courses, a generic HR diploma, an MBA-style theory course, Python, the PL-300 exam, ten AI certificates, and trying to memorise every ATS.

Ferra dashboard — what “done” looks like.

  1. Grey source tabs as Tables (snapshot, tracker, funnel, resignations, interviews).
  2. A hidden Filter Engine: role and month drop-downs, * / >0 criteria, match flags, ranks.

Then: hire 20 engineers in Europe.

This is the answer to “you have hired manufacturing people, why should we let you hire software?” It is a case, not a claim that she has already done it. Label it as a project.

Brief. A European product company needs 20 engineers in six months: 8 backend, 6 frontend, 3 DevOps, 3 data. Locations: Dublin, Amsterdam, Berlin. She produces all of the following.

  1. Workforce note: why these 20, and what slips if hiring slips.
  2. Four short job descriptions, written the way a European company writes them.
  3. A candidate persona for each role, including who is a false match.
  4. Boolean strings and one X-ray string per role.
  5. Channel plan: LinkedIn, GitHub, referrals, communities. What she will not waste time on.
  6. A one-page market comparison of Dublin, Amsterdam and Berlin: supply, language, visa, and pay.
  7. A salary band for each role, with the source named and dated.
  8. A funnel: how many screens, interviews and offers she needs for 20 joiners, and the assumption behind each step.
  9. The Excel tracker, filled with realistic dummy data. No real candidate personal data.
  10. The Power BI dashboard built on that file.
  11. An interview loop: who meets the candidate, in what order, and what “no” looks like.
  12. A GDPR note: what she stores, for how long, and what she will not put into a public AI tool.
  13. A one-page note to the hiring director: forecast, risks, and the decision she needs from them.

Every application needs a sponsor filter. Country, visa mentioned or not, salary if shown, and whether the employer is a known sponsor (IND recognised sponsor in the Netherlands; employment-permit employer in Ireland). EURES and Indeed list jobs. They do not give a non-EU citizen the right to take the job. Fifteen to twenty-five carefully chosen applications a week beat two hundred Easy Applies.

Interviews

Twenty stories, told in English, with numbers.

Each story needs the situation, what she decided, what she did herself, and the result. If she cannot remember the result, she reconstructs it from old trackers or she drops the story. Speaking them aloud matters more than writing them perfectly.

  1. The hardest role she closed at Ferra, and why it was hard.
  2. A bulk-hiring push at Skillvert or BYJU’S that she actually ran.
  3. A hiring manager who wanted a candidate who does not exist.
  4. An offer the candidate declined, and what she changed next.
  5. A compensation conversation where the band was wrong.
  6. Candidates going quiet, and the follow-up that worked.
  7. A role that had to be filled in a very short window.
  8. A pipeline that looked busy and was actually empty.
  9. A time she shortened time-to-fill, with the before and after.
  10. A sourcing channel she stopped using, and the evidence.
  1. Disagreement with a senior stakeholder. What she held, what she conceded.
  2. Several roles at once, and how she chose where to spend the day.
  3. A metric she showed a manager, and the decision it caused.
  4. A hire that turned out poorly. What she would screen differently.
  5. A candidate she kept informed when the process was slow.
  6. A process she changed: scheduling, feedback, or the tracker.
  7. A technical role she did not understand on day one. How she learned it.
  8. A moment she changed a hiring manager’s mind.
  9. How she widened a shortlist beyond the obvious profile.
  10. A failure at BYJU’S, Skillvert or Ferra, and the rule she kept.
English is the job. Screening calls, pushback, pay conversations and a five-minute readout of a funnel all happen in English. If that is not already easy, thirty minutes of speaking practice every day outranks another online course. Record the twenty stories. Listen back once.
